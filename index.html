7

What did you discover?

Compare at least two sample sizes in Step 6 to unlock.

Answer each question on your own first, then check.

1. Did the population histogram in Step 1 change when you changed n?
No. The individual scores and their spread, σ = 86.80, stay the same. Larger samples do not reduce the spread of individual GMAT scores.
2. What happened to the spread of the 30 sample means as n increased?
It shrank. The standard error of the mean is σx = σ / √n: about 38.82 at n = 5, 19.41 at n = 20, and 12.28 at n = 50. Your observed values (STDEV.S of the 30 means) should be close to these.
3. Where was the histogram of sample means centered?
Near μ = 520.78 for every n. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator: E(x) = μ.
4. What happened to the shape of the sampling distribution as n increased?
It became more bell-shaped (closer to normal), even though the population is right-skewed. The bonus with 1,000 samples in Step 4 shows this most clearly.
5. Going from n = 5 to n = 20 multiplies the sample size by 4. What happens to the standard error?
It is cut in half, because √4 = 2: 86.80 / √5 ≈ 38.82 and 86.80 / √20 ≈ 19.41. Quadrupling n halves σx.
Central Limit Theorem (CLT). If a random sample of size n is drawn from a population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the distribution of the sample mean X approaches a normal distribution with mean μ and standard error σx = σ / √n as the sample size increases.

Rule of thumb: n ≥ 30 is sufficient for most populations, even skewed ones. Symmetric populations require a smaller n.

"The raw data did not become normal. The sample means did."
